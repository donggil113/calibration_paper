\paragraph{What this changes about external validation.}
The standard package of evidence --- an AUROC obtained in a second country ---
is not wrong, it is answering a different question from the one deployment
poses.  Our transfer matrix reproduces the reassuring AUROC and shows
calibration failing behind it.  Any use of a model that is not pure ranking
depends on the half that goes unreported.  We propose that a deployment dossier
state, per site and per label, the calibration ratio, the decomposition, and the
label count required to close the gap --- all of which are computable with the
released toolbox, and two of which need no target labels at all.

\paragraph{What a hospital should actually do.}
Our results do not support the workflow we set out to justify.  We expected the
unlabeled goodness-of-fit test to license a free prevalence correction at sites
that pass it.  It does not: the correction applied with an unlabeled prevalence
estimate was worse than shipping unchanged at every site here, the test cannot
see the shift direction responsible, and no gating or shrinkage rule we tried
repaired it.  We report that because a method that fails in the direction of
overconfidence is exactly the kind a deployment guideline must not contain.

What the results do support is budget-dependent, and the dependence is the
point.  Below roughly fifty adjudicated cases, ship the model unchanged and
report its output as ranks rather than as risks.  This is not caution for its
own sake: at that budget a fitted map was worse than doing nothing in a majority
of site--label pairs, and cross-validated selection did not rescue it, because
the evidence needed to decide whether to act is the same scarce evidence that
would be used to act.  There is no procedure that manufactures information that
is not there.

With a few hundred cases, use cross-validated selection over a candidate set
that includes doing nothing.  By that budget it both acts where a cliff exists
and abstains where one does not, and it improves the tail of the outcome
distribution --- the bad-day number, which is what a deployment actually risks
--- as well as the average.

The uncomfortable part of this is worth stating directly: none of these steps is
free, and the step we hoped would be --- inferring the target prevalence from
unlabeled data --- is the one that fails.  What we can offer instead is that the
labelled requirement is small, bounded in advance, and spent on a decision as
much as on a fit.

\paragraph{Implications for LMIC deployment.}
The honest reading is more demanding than the one we anticipated, and still
tractable.  The prevalence component is the large and correctable share of the
cliff, and prevalence is exactly what differs most between a tertiary centre in
a high-income country and a primary-care or screening setting elsewhere --- so
the available gain is largest precisely where deployment is most likely.  But it
cannot be claimed without labels.  The requirement a site must plan for is a
few dozen adjudicated tracings, which is a chart-review afternoon rather than a
research programme, and it is bounded in advance rather than open-ended.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\emph{Label provenance.}  Most of these cohorts take their labels from an
interpretation of the ECG, so a cross-country difference in labels can always
be partly a difference in reading practice rather than in patients.  The
EchoNext arm, whose labels come from echocardiography, is the control for this,
and it is the one arm we regard as decisive on the point.

\emph{The Korean cohort.}  Its inclusion requires institutional approval and a
data transfer agreement.  The federated protocol removes the technical
obstacle, not the governance one.

\emph{$\gamma$ is an assumption.}  Both the partial-identification interval and
the conformal inflation depend on a concept-shift budget that cannot be
estimated from the data it is used on.  We report sweeps rather than single
numbers, and $\gamma_{\min}$ as the data's own floor; a reader who disbelieves
our $\gamma$ can read their own off the sweep.

\emph{Conditional versus unconditional test size.}  The unlabeled test is
calibrated by parametric bootstrap and has close to nominal unconditional size.
In deployment, though, every site uses \emph{one} source operator estimated by
whoever shipped the model, and conditional on a particular such operator the
size can be materially higher.  This is why we recommend the minimax
correction, which involves no binary decision, over the gate.  A vendor
shipping a model for this workflow should estimate the source operator on as
much data as possible and report its conditioning.

\emph{Simulation versus cohorts.}  The theorems are validated on a generator
with known ground truth because the split they concern is unobservable in real
data.  The empirical claims about transfer are separate and belong to the
cohorts; we have not used the simulator to support them, and the two are never
pooled in one table.

\paragraph{What would falsify this.}
A site with a large prevalence shift where correcting with the \emph{measured}
target prevalence fails to recover most of the calibration error would
contradict Theorem~\ref{thm:decomp}(c) as applied here.  A site where the
measured label requirement materially exceeds the plug-in bound at its measured
$\Gamma$ would contradict Theorem~\ref{thm:samplesize}(a).

Our own negative result is the most checkable claim in the paper and the one we
would most like tested.  If, on real cohorts, an unlabeled prevalence estimate
does recover the label-shift component --- if the shift there is off-cone where
ours was on-cone --- then the cheaper workflow we could not justify becomes
available, and we would want that known.  Everything needed to check it is in
the released code, which is the point of releasing it.
